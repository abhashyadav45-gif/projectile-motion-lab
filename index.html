    The physics behind it

    Without air resistance, the horizontal velocity stays constant and gravity acts only downward. With launch speed v₀, angle θ, launch height h₀ and gravity g:

    x = v₀ cosθ · t y = h₀ + v₀ sinθ · t − ½ g t² T = ( v₀ sinθ + √( (v₀ sinθ)² + 2 g h₀ ) ) / g H = h₀ + (v₀ sinθ)² / (2g)     R = v₀ cosθ · T

    When launched from the ground (h₀ = 0), R = v₀² sin2θ / g, so 45° gives the longest range. Try 30° and 60°: they land at the same place.

    With air resistance, drag opposes the motion and grows with the square of the speed: F = ½ ρ Cd A v². The acceleration is a = −k |v| v − g ĵ, where k = ρ Cd A / (2m). There is no simple closed-form answer, so the simulator solves it numerically with the 4th-order Runge–Kutta method (time step 0.01 s). It uses ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ and Cd = 0.47 (smooth sphere). On the Moon and other bodies, the drag setting is only a what-if, because the air density stays at the Earth value.

    Things to try: find the best angle with drag on (it drops below 45°), compare a light and a heavy ball of the same size, and keep several paths to compare them on one graph.
